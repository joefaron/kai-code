Why an airboat

The obvious build is a submerged propeller on a shaft coming out of the back of the hull. It is also the hardest thing on this list to make waterproof: the shaft has to spin and keep water out, which needs a stuffing tube, grease and a lot of fiddling. One leak and the motor and battery are done.

An airboat deletes that whole problem. The propellers sit in the air above the deck. Nothing rotates below the waterline, so there is no seal to fail and nothing that can shed plastic into the water. The beach is flat and protected, which is the one condition where an airboat is actually the better boat — it would be a bad choice in surf or wind chop.

The trade-off, said plainly: an airboat is slower and more wind-sensitive than a prop boat, and two spinning props need guards so nobody's fingers get near them. If the race is a straight sprint and there's a breeze, a submerged prop wins. We think a boat that definitely runs beats a boat that is theoretically faster.

Steering with no rudder

Two motors, one on each side. Run them at different speeds and the boat turns — same idea as a tank or a skid-steer. That removes the rudder, the servo, the linkage and a second failure point. The tablet interface on the Control page already does the math: one thumb position becomes two motor speeds.

left  = throttle + steering
right = throttle - steering
(then clamp both to -100 .. 100)

How the tablet talks to the boat

There is no Wi-Fi at the beach, and there doesn't need to be. The ESP32 runs in Access Point mode: the boat is the Wi-Fi network. The tablet joins SS-JACKOLANTERN-v2.2, opens http://192.168.4.1, and the control page is served by the boat itself. No internet, no router, no phone hotspot.

📶 Why Wi-Fi and not Bluetooth

Bluetooth sounds like the obvious choice for a remote control, and it's the wrong one here. ESP32 Wi-Fi reaches roughly 150–240 m line-of-sight; plain BLE is more like 10–30 m. On top of that, an Android tablet's browser cannot open a classic Bluetooth serial connection at all — only BLE GATT, through a fussy Web Bluetooth permission flow, which would mean writing an actual app. Wi-Fi AP needs no app, no pairing and no install: join the network, open the page.

The thing that actually costs range is water. 2.4 GHz is absorbed by it, so mount the ESP32 a few inches up on a mast rather than flat on the deck, and don't put it in a metal or foil-lined box. That matters more than the radio you pick.

Hull

  • Shape: flat-bottom punt / catamaran. Two sealed 2L bottles as side pontoons under a corrugated-plastic deck is the cheapest stable platform that exists, and it is genuinely recycled material.
  • Size: about 14–18 in long. Big enough to carry the battery box steadily, small enough to carry to the beach in one hand.
  • Buoyancy math: 1 liter of displaced water holds up about 1 kg. Two 2L bottles = ~4 kg of lift. The whole boat will weigh well under 1 kg, so it floats high even swamped — that's the "floats independently" criterion, proven with numbers.
  • Halloween: jack-o'-lantern face on the bow, a small LED inside on the ESP32, black + orange deck. Costs nothing and it's a judged category.

Will it actually work? The numbers

We couldn't find anyone who had published measured thrust for this exact little motor and prop, so these are physics estimates — and the first job when the parts arrive is to replace every one of them with a real measurement (see the tests below).

QuestionEstimateHow we got it
Push from one fan~9–17 g Momentum theory for an 80 mm prop turning 0.25–0.6 W of motor power into moving air.
Top speed, calm water~0.5–0.8 mph Where two fans' push equals the water drag on two half-sunk bottles. A slow walk: about 25 m in 70–100 seconds.
Headwind that stops it dead~10 mph Air drag on everything above the water grows with wind speed squared. At 5 mph it barely matters; at 10 mph it cancels the fans.
Crosswind that could tip it~30+ mph Wind's tipping force vs. the boat's weight acting across a 20 cm-wide catamaran. 10× safety margin at 10 mph, 2.6× at 20 mph.

What the numbers say: this boat will not tip over — the catamaran is the right call. Its real enemy is being pushed backwards by a headwind, not capsizing. So every design choice below is about staying low, light and small above the water.

Should it have a sail?

No. A sail only helps when the wind blows the way you want to go, and we don't know which way the race course runs. On any other heading it pushes the boat sideways, fights the steering, and adds exactly the thing the numbers say is dangerous: more area above the water. The fans are our sail — one that points wherever we want.

The one sail worth building is the backup: a small folded sail in the bag that clips on if the electronics die at the beach, so the boat still finishes.

The layout

  • Pontoons wide apart: bottle centres about 20 cm (8 in) apart. Wider = harder to tip, but harder to turn. 20 cm is the balance.
  • Heaviest thing lowest and in the middle: the power bank goes flat on the deck, centred between the bottles. Never up high.
  • Fans at the back, as far apart as the deck allows: the further apart they are, the more turning force the same speed difference makes. Mount them just above the deck — high enough to clear spray, low enough not to act like a sail.
  • Air must blow backwards: if a fan blows the wrong way, swap its two wires. Don't fix it in the code.
  • Brain box on a short riser: the ESP32 is light, so a few inches up is free — and it's better for Wi-Fi range.
  • Two small fins under the back: a flat boat skids sideways in a breeze. A 3 cm plastic fin under the back of each bottle keeps it tracking straight. Test with and without — fins also make it turn more slowly.

Tests to run when the parts arrive

  1. Thrust (kitchen scale): tape one motor and prop to a small block, blowing air up, standing on a kitchen scale. Zero the scale, turn it on: the reading goes up by the thrust in grams. Try 1 prop vs the other kind. Write down the numbers.
  2. Power bank stays on: ESP32 plugged in, motors off, 10 minutes. Some banks switch themselves off when not much power is being used.
  3. Leak test: paper towel in the closed, empty box, hold it under water for 60 seconds. Dry towel = pass.
  4. Tip test: in the bathtub, push down on one side with a finger on the kitchen scale until the other bottle lifts. More grams = more stable.
  5. Wind test: hair dryer on cool, from the front, then the side. Does it hold its heading? Does it drift? Try with and without the fins.
  6. Speed test: time it over 2 m in a pool or the tub. Compare with the estimate above — were we right?

💡 Fail-safe to build in from day one

The ESP32 sketch stops both motors if it hasn't heard from the tablet in 800 ms. Walk out of range and the boat coasts to a stop instead of driving off across the harbor. Also tie 30 ft of fishing line to the bow for the first water tests.

Waterproofing: what actually has to be dry

This is the part people get backwards. Because it's an airboat, nothing is submerged and nothing spins through the hull — so the box does not have to survive being underwater. It has to survive splash and one capsize, long enough to get the boat back. That is a much easier problem, and it is why the design is built this way.

  • Dry box: a latching IP67 project box with a rubber gasket and a clear lid. The clear lid matters twice: judges can see the electronics, and Kai can see at a glance whether water got in.
  • The only holes: four motor wires leaving the box. Drill undersized, push the wires through, fill with marine silicone or hot glue, and strain-relieve them — zip-tie the wire to the box inside so a tug can't pull the seal open.
  • Mount it high: the box goes on the deck, not down between the pontoons where spray collects. It's also better for Wi-Fi range.
  • Plastic only: ABS is invisible to 2.4 GHz. Never put the ESP32 in a metal or foil-lined box — that's a Faraday cage and the range goes to nothing.
  • Batteries live outside: the 6×AA pack is wider than the box's interior, and you want to swap cells without breaking the seal anyway. Zip-loc bag, taped shut, velcroed to the deck. If it gets wet you've lost a $1 pack, not the boat.
  • Test it empty: before any electronics go in, put a folded paper towel in the closed box and hold it under water for 60 seconds. Dry towel = you're done. This is a real engineering test and it belongs in the write-up.

🔌 Power: a USB power bank, not a battery pack

The first plan was a 6×AA NiMH pack. Then we found a spare USB-C power bank in a CrunchLabs Hack Pack box, and it is not just cheaper — it is better engineering. A battery pack’s voltage sags the instant the motors pull current, and if it sags far enough the ESP32’s regulator gives up and the board reboots mid-drive. (That trap is real: a rechargeable NiMH cell is 1.2 V, not 1.5 V, so a 4×AA pack is 4.8 V, not 6 V — right on the edge.) A power bank puts out a regulated 5 V no matter what the motors do, so the problem simply stops existing.

Wiring: one port runs a normal USB cable to the ESP32. The second port runs a cut USB cable — red to the driver’s VM, black to its GND. Both ports share the bank’s ground, so the grounds are already tied.

Update (Sep 19): the motors now run from our old 8×AA holder, loaded with 6 AA + 2 dummy cells = 9 V (about 2× the push of 5 V). Eight real AA would be 12 V, over the driver’s 10.8 V limit. The ESP32 stays on the power bank, so the extra volts never reach it.

The one thing to test first: some power banks switch themselves off when the current drawn drops below about 50–100 mA. An ESP32 running a Wi-Fi access point draws roughly 120–160 mA, which normally keeps the bank awake — but leave it idling on the bench for ten minutes and make sure it stays on before you trust it on the water. If it cuts out under motor start-up instead, one 1000 µF capacitor across the 5 V line fixes it.
